Answer Accuracy Rate is the share of checkable claims about a brand in AI answers — price, locations, product scope, eligibility, contact routes — that match the brand's own maintained fact sheet. It is scored claim by claim, not as one verdict per answer, and separately from tone: a positive-sounding answer with a wrong price, location or product scope is a risk, not a win.
Key takeaways
  • Answer Accuracy Rate = correct brand claims divided by checkable brand claims, scored against a maintained fact sheet.
  • Score claim by claim; one verdict per answer cannot be routed to a fix.
  • A positive answer with a wrong price, location or product scope counts as a risk, not a win.
  • Count wrong and missing claims separately; a fact is missing only if the prompt required it, and critical errors are reported next to the unweighted rate.

Consensus definition

Answer accuracy is the degree to which the factual claims in an AI-generated answer agree with a trusted reference. Research measures it claim by claim: FActScore breaks a long-form generation into atomic facts and reports the percentage supported by a reliable knowledge source; on biographies, ChatGPT (2023) scored only 58 %1. Editorial audits rate whole answers: in a study led by the BBC and the EBU, journalists from 22 public-service media organisations rated each answer of four assistants to news questions per criterion and found significant accuracy problems in 20 % of 2,709 rated answers, with outdated information among the most frequent errors2. Since July 2026 vendors have offered claim checks against a customer-maintained knowledge base3. Applied to brand monitoring, we define Answer Accuracy Rate as the number of correct brand claims divided by the number of checkable brand claims, where checkable means the claim can be compared with a value on the brand's own fact sheet.

rhinegold operator refinement

Rhinegold's refinement: accuracy is scored against a maintained brand fact sheet, claim by claim — never as an overall impression of the answer. The fact sheet lists each claim the brand needs to be right: price or price range, locations served, product scope and exclusions, eligibility rules, contact routes. Each row carries the valid value, the date it was last confirmed and the page that proves it. Every claim about the brand in an answer is then scored as correct, wrong, outdated or unverifiable against that sheet. Two rules follow. First, a positive-sounding answer with a wrong price, location or product scope is a risk, not a win — tone and accuracy are scored on separate axes and never netted against each other. Second, a wrong claim and a missing claim are counted separately, because they need different fixes: a wrong claim points to a source to correct; a missing claim calls for a check whether the fact is published, accessible, unambiguous and current. A fact counts as missing only if it was set in advance as required for that prompt — an answer about delivery regions does not miss the founding year — and only in an answer that otherwise responds, which is not the same as a refusal. Refusal rates do not settle the question: assistants rarely refused news questions (0.5 % in the EBU/BBC study2), while in one agency's study of its own brand, answers to comparison prompts carried no brand information 80 % of the time4. Under binary grading a wrong answer and an abstention both score zero, which rewards guessing over abstaining5. The headline rate stays unweighted; errors on critical facts — price, availability, product scope, eligibility — are reported separately, so a wrong price is never averaged away by a correct founding year.

Operational use

Build and version the fact sheet first: one row per claim, with valid value, source page, last-confirmed date and a flag for critical facts (price, availability, product scope, eligibility). Run a fixed prompt set per assistant on a schedule — the same prompts each round — and set, for each prompt, the fact-sheet rows its answer must contain. Extract every brand claim from the answers and report side by side: the unweighted accuracy rate on checkable claims, the critical errors listed separately, the split of errors into wrong versus outdated, the share of answers in which a fact required for that prompt was missing, and coverage — the share of brand claims in the answers that map to a fact-sheet row. Before treating a missing fact as a publishing gap, check whether the fact is already published, accessible, unambiguous and current. Route each error to a fix: an outdated claim points to a stale page or third-party listing; the same wrong claim across several assistants points to a source they share. Read the rate next to AI Answer Sentiment, so that a warm answer with a wrong fact is flagged rather than reported as a success.

Measurement boundary

The rate covers only claims that are on the fact sheet: anything the sheet does not list cannot be scored, and a stale sheet turns correct answers into false errors. It is measured on a sampled prompt set, not on what real buyers asked, and answers vary between runs, so a single round is a snapshot. Automated claim extraction and checking can carry the volume — FActScore's automated estimate of the overall score came within 2 points of the human score1 — but that calibration was done on biographies, not on brand facts, so a sample of automated verdicts still needs human review. Public benchmarks are no substitute: summarisation leaderboards measure faithfulness to a supplied document6 and the leaderboard is updated as models change7; news audits measure news questions28. The one published brand-level measurement we found — one agency testing 1,562 prompts about its own brand on six AI platforms, May–June 2026 — rated 95.9 % of answers that contained brand information as accurate; answers without brand information came mostly from comparison prompts4. It is a single-brand vendor study; we found no cross-brand baseline for a good rate. The rate also cannot show whether a buyer saw the wrong claim or acted on it.

Distinct from

From Hallucination: hallucination names the failure — false or fabricated content presented as true; Answer Accuracy Rate is the scored measure, and it also counts outdated and correct claims, not only fabrications. From AI Answer Sentiment and Multi-Axis Sentiment: sentiment scores how the brand is framed, accuracy scores whether the stated facts are true — an endorsing answer can contain a wrong price. From Negated Mention: an answer that advises against the brand can be fully accurate, and a recommending one can be wrong. From Phantom URL: a phantom URL is a fabricated source address, an error in what the answer cites; accuracy covers the claims in the answer text. From LLM Brand Tracking: tracking is the monitoring discipline; accuracy is one dimension it reports, next to presence and framing. From Grounded Response Rate: an answer that retrieved sources can still reproduce them wrongly.

Empirical anchor

Stated certainty is a poor guide to correctness. In the Tow Center's test of eight AI search tools — 1,600 queries asking for the source of a news excerpt — more than 60 % of answers were wrong, and ChatGPT misidentified 134 articles but signalled a lack of confidence only 15 times in 200 responses910. In BBC companion research, 42 % of UK adults said they would trust an original news source less if an AI summary of it contained errors2. These studies show why accuracy matters, not how well a brand metric works: they measure news questions and attribution, and transferring the pattern to brands is an inference that a brand's own fact-sheet check has to confirm.

Common mistakes

  • Counting a positive or recommending answer as a win without checking the facts in it — a warm answer with a wrong price or a product the brand does not offer is a liability.
  • Scoring the whole answer as right or wrong — one verdict per answer hides which claim failed and cannot be routed to a fix.
  • Checking against memory or the live website instead of a versioned fact sheet — without dated valid values, an outdated claim cannot be told apart from a wrong one, and rounds are not comparable.
  • Treating a missing fact and a wrong fact the same — they need different fixes, and a missing fact in an answer that otherwise responds is not the same as a refusal.
  • Counting every fact-sheet row absent from an answer as missing — only facts set in advance as required for that prompt count, and a missing fact may already be published and simply not found.
  • Using public hallucination leaderboards as a proxy for what assistants say about the brand — they measure faithfulness to a supplied document, not brand facts.

Where consensus is missing

There is no standard definition of a brand claim, no shared taxonomy for wrong, outdated and unverifiable claims, and no public benchmark of brand-fact accuracy across brands that we could find. Published audits cover news questions and summarisation; the transfer to brand facts is a practitioner inference, not a measured result.
