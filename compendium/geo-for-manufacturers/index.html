GEO for Manufacturers

GEO for manufacturers applies generative engine optimization to companies whose products reach buyers through distributors, resellers and marketplaces. When a buyer asks an AI system a specification question, the answer can be assembled from those intermediaries' product pages and data sheets instead of the maker's own site. In our working method, the first metric is how often the maker, not its reseller, is named and cited for specification-level prompts.

Consensus definition

There is no settled industry definition. The term describes generative engine optimization applied to companies that make physical products and sell part or all of their volume through distribution. An illustration: a buyer asks an AI assistant for the rated torque of a gearbox by its part number; the answer gives the right value, names the maker and cites a distributor's product page. The maker is visible, but the source and the specification the buyer reads belong to the distributor, and the source link leads there; whether anyone clicks it is not measured. For unbranded prompts, generative answers draw mostly on sources the company does not own: according to Bain, citing ScrunchAI data on about 500 million citations, 89% of unbranded prompts are fulfilled by third-party sources1. Whether a reseller page counts as a third-party source depends on the classification: Bain separates brand-owned from non-brand-owned media without naming resellers1, and Chen et al. count manufacturer and retailer domains together as "brand" in their vertical experiments2. We count the maker's sales channel as a class of its own and report it separately. The product-data standards already separate the two roles: schema.org describes the maker and its product through the manufacturer, brand, mpn (manufacturer part number) and gtin properties, while sku is a merchant-specific identifier3.

rhinegold operator refinement

Rhinegold's refinement: for a manufacturer, being named is not the same as being the source. A distributor page that names the product counts as a mention under a plain mention rate, yet the citation and the specification values the buyer reads belong to the distributor, and the source link leads to its page — whether anyone clicks is not measured. We therefore record two facts for each answer to a specification-level prompt (part number, standard, rated value, compatibility, replacement): whether the maker is named, and which domain types are cited — the maker's own, another maker's, reseller, marketplace, trade publication or data-sheet host, none. "Cited" here means at least one cited URL on the maker's own domain; under the mention-with-source reading in Citation Rate a reseller citation would also count. The analyses are derived from the two facts, not from a fixed list of combined states. The first metric is the share of answers in which the maker is named and its own domain is cited — maker named-and-cited share on specification prompts — reported per prompt across runs, next to the share of answers that cite a reseller or marketplace and the share that cite the maker's domain without naming the maker, not merged into one visibility score. This split is our working method, not an industry standard, and the thesis that specification answers are mostly assembled from intermediaries is a hypothesis to test per product category, not a finding. A reseller citation is also not automatically a loss: where a partner holds the sale, decide per product line which domain should carry the citation before optimizing for it.

Operational use

Use it when a manufacturer sells through distribution and needs to know whether AI answers cite its own pages. (1) Build a prompt set from specification questions — part numbers, standards, rated values, compatibility and replacement — plus the category's discovery prompts. (2) Run it on each engine you track, several times per prompt, and record for every answer whether the maker is named and which domain types are cited. (3) Make each product resolvable on the maker's site: an indexed page per product that is eligible to be shown with a snippet, the condition Google sets for supporting links in its AI features4, with specification values in text and structured data that states manufacturer, brand, mpn and gtin and carries spec values as property-value pairs3, matching what the page shows4. Google's merchant listing experiences are open only to pages where a shopper can buy the product5, while separate product-snippet markup covers product pages without a purchase option6. State sources, figures and quotable statements on those pages; citing sources, adding quotations and adding statistics were the strongest methods in the GEO benchmark, with 30–40% relative improvement on the paper's position-adjusted word-count measure, on general queries7; a later benchmark of conversational-SEO methods across question answering and product recommendation found most methods it tested largely ineffective and often harmful for document ranking8. Neither result has been tested on specification prompts. (4) Give distributors the same identifiers and values, not identical page copy: Bing documents that AI systems group near-duplicate pages and choose one to represent them9. Where the maker's page should carry the citation and contracts allow, ask partners for a canonical link (Bing gives this advice for syndicated content9). Submit the identifiers and values to Google Manufacturer Center, which takes GTIN, descriptions and optional specification attributes by feed10. (5) Work the independent third-party layer — trade publications, association and standards directories, application case studies — as earned media grounding, because cited sources are mostly not brand-owned1. Re-run the same prompt set after each change and compare the maker named-and-cited share, not single answers; keep a control set of product lines you do not change, run each prompt several times, and note engine or model changes between runs.

Measurement boundary

No platform reports whether an answer credited the maker or its reseller; naming and cited domain types have to be coded from sampled answers. With part numbers and variants, the prompt space is far larger than any set can cover, so results describe the set, not the market, and answers vary between runs (measurement consistency). Google's documentation places Manufacturer Center data in Shopping ads and free listings10; we found no statement that it feeds AI answers. The GEO benchmark reports no result for a manufacturing or product-specification category7, so its uplift figures are untested for this setting. A competing reading is that specification prompts often name the maker or a part number and then mostly cite the maker's own domain; the coding above is built to decide between the two. The buying side has moved into AI answers: in a Semrush survey of US B2B professionals (March and April 2026), 92% of the 519 respondents who use AI tools at work said AI had shaped their vendor shortlist11, and Bain reports that buyers at small and medium-size businesses have started to build vendor shortlists inside LLMs1. These figures do not transfer cleanly: Bain's US consumer survey (n=1,500, September 2025) found that 44% of online buyers mostly start in an LLM or split their search between AI tools and search engines1; Semrush's sample was 15% manufacturing, and its findings rest on the respondents who use AI at work11; G2's figure that 51% of buyers now start research with an AI chatbot more often than with Google, up from 29% in its 2025 report, covers B2B software buyers only12. None of the three reports a separate result for manufacturing buyers. Bain's 89% third-party figure rests on proprietary ScrunchAI data that cannot be reproduced1. Finally, a reseller citation can still end in a sale of the maker's product: citation share does not measure revenue.

Distinct from

From GEO in general: GEO asks whether a brand is surfaced and cited; for a manufacturer the competing sources include its own distributors, so the question becomes which domain in the sales chain receives the citation. From discovery prompts: those ask an AI for options ("which suppliers make X"), where being named is the goal; specification prompts ask about a product's values, part number or replacement, where the maker can be named and still not be cited. From structured data: markup states who made the product, but Google says its AI features require no special markup4; we found no documentation of how much markup weighs in citation selection. From earned media grounding: that works with independent third parties; distributors are third parties the maker supplies with product data, so their pages can be aligned, not only earned. From the invisible shortlist: that describes a vendor missing from the AI's answer; here the product can be present while the maker is not the cited source.

Common mistakes

  • Counting a product mention on a distributor or marketplace page as maker visibility: the answer names the product, but the citation and its link go to the reseller.
  • Keeping specification values only in configurators, images or PDF data sheets: values locked in images or configurators appear on no indexed page, and PDFs, though indexable, have not been shown to be eligible as supporting links in AI features.
  • Expecting AI-specific schema to win citations. Structured data identifies maker and part number; it has to match the visible page, and Google states that its AI features need no special markup.
  • Treating every reseller citation as a loss. Where a partner holds the sale, the partner's page can be the right citation target; set the target per product line before optimizing.
  • Carrying buyer-survey figures from software or consumer samples over to industrial purchasing, or applying the GEO benchmark's uplift figures to specification queries for which it reported no results.
