AI Content Governance

AI Content Governance is the set of rules, roles and review steps by which a company decides which AI-generated marketing content may be published, and who answers for it when it is wrong or off-brand. It concerns what the company itself publishes with AI — not what AI systems say about the company.

Consensus definition

AI Content Governance covers four questions: who owns AI-generated content and its errors; which content needs human approval before publication; how much variation from the brand voice is acceptable; and which guardrails sit inside the tools rather than in a policy document. The NIST Generative AI Profile frames the first two as governance tasks: organisations should "define organizational responsibilities for periodic review of content provenance and incident monitoring" and "review and test AI-generated content against these guidelines"1. It adds that generative AI use "may also warrant additional human review, tracking and documentation, and greater management oversight"1. Accountability does not move to the tool: in Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024) a Canadian tribunal held that the airline "is responsible for all the information on its website", whether it "comes from a static page or a chatbot"2. In the EU, Article 50 of the AI Act has applied since 2 August 20263. Deployers must disclose deep fakes, and must disclose AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest — unless the text "has undergone a process of human review or editorial control" and a person "holds editorial responsibility for the publication"3. Providers of generating systems must mark output in a machine-readable way; for systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 the Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) moved that deadline to 2 December 20264. The Commission's Code of Practice on marking and labelling is voluntary5; its guidelines of 20 July 2026 define the scope of Article 506. In the US, the FTC rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, in effect since 21 October 2024, reaches "AI-generated fake reviews"78.

rhinegold operator refinement

Rhinegold's reframe: most governance programmes measure the process — how many pieces were reviewed, how long approval took. The outcome sits outside the company: content published at volume becomes source material that AI systems retrieve and summarise, so off-brand or wrong AI-generated pages can resurface in answers about the brand. A governance programme is therefore incomplete until it checks, on a fixed schedule, whether AI answers still describe the brand as intended — the Brand Voice Match and sentiment drift of the brand in AI answers are the closest external read-outs. This link between own AI content and third-party AI answers is a working assumption, not a measured effect.

Operational use

Set up governance in five steps. (1) Ownership: name one accountable owner per content type and settle in advance who decides when marketing, legal, compliance and IT disagree. (2) Approval tiers by risk: full human review with a named editor for claims about prices, terms, products and anything that may count as public-interest text under Article 503; sampled review for low-risk variants such as ad copy permutations. (3) Brand tolerance: write down what may vary (length, tone within a range, examples) and what may not (claims, product names, legal wording), and test generated variants against that list1. (4) Guardrails in the tools: blocked terms, required disclaimers and approved source material configured in the generation tool itself, so the rule applies before anyone reviews. (5) Records: log what was generated, who reviewed it and when it was published — without a record, a claim of "human review or editorial control"3 cannot be shown later. Then check the outcome: track how AI answers describe the brand over time (with a consistent method) and how often its own pages are cited, for example in the Bing AI Performance report. Which marketing texts count as public-interest publications under Article 50 is a question for the Commission's guidelines6 and legal counsel; this entry does not settle it.

Measurement boundary

Process metrics — share of content reviewed, approval time, number of escalations — show that governance runs, not that it works. Outcome signals in AI answers cannot be attributed to a company's own AI-generated content alone: answers draw on many sources, and the effect of one page is not observable in isolation. Legal duties differ by jurisdiction and are still being specified (the EU Code of Practice is voluntary5, and the 50(2) transition runs to 2 December 20264). This entry describes governance practice; it is not legal advice.

Distinct from

From AI Reputation Risk: that entry covers the exposure from what AI systems say about a brand; AI Content Governance covers what the company publishes with AI. They meet at Article 50, which both entries cite: labelling duties for AI content. From Brand Voice Match: a measure of how faithfully AI answers render a brand's voice — usable as an outcome check for governance, not a governance process itself. From Hallucination: wrong statements about a brand in third-party AI answers. When a company's own chatbot or generator states something wrong, the company is the publisher; in Moffatt v. Air Canada the airline was held responsible for its chatbot's statement2.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the AI tool or its vendor as the responsible party: the tribunal in Moffatt v. Air Canada called the airline's argument that its chatbot was a separate entity "a remarkable submission" and held the airline responsible2.
  • Reviewing every output with the same effort: uniform review either stalls high-volume variants or waves high-risk claims through; tier review by risk instead.
  • Assuming the voluntary Code of Practice settles legal compliance: the Code is voluntary5, and the Article 50 duties apply regardless of whether a company signs it3.
  • Keeping no record of review: the Article 50(4) exception for public-interest text depends on human review or editorial control and named editorial responsibility3, which needs documentation to be shown.
  • Measuring governance only by internal process figures: none of them shows whether AI answers about the brand stay accurate and on-brand.
